Richemont — the case for an AI-native PMO

Prep for Lillian's second interview. The frame: the modern transformation program is run from a data-driven PMO that uses AI to keep a group of vendors aligned to the business's capital plan. That is the twist on a classic hire — and it is what Richemont is actually recruiting for.

The one-line frame

Richemont's Bluefield S/4HANA programme (New Foundations, template Gemini) is a multi-vendor transformation: SAP as the platform, a Systems Integrator as the channel partner delivering it, Salesforce and Google Cloud on the client seam, Workday and Cegid at the edges. Every one of those vendors is important. None of them is impartial. No single SI delivers a transformation of this size on its own — it is always a group of vendors, each with its own interests, that has to be aligned to the business's outcomes.

Aligning that group, on behalf of the business, is what Lillian is an expert at. That is the seat Richemont is trying to fill.

What is different in 2026

A program plan built the way IKEA's was built five-plus years ago is no longer the standard. Modern transformations are run from a PMO that:

  • Consolidates every input received so far into a structured, AI-accessible repository — vendor decks, workshop outputs, workstream notes, prior-phase artifacts, interview material — one surface, queryable by any AI tool the PMO chooses to use.
  • Uses AI as the operating instrument of program leadership, not as a downstream deliverable owned by a vendor. Decision intelligence is how the PMO stays on top of a program with twenty Maisons, hundreds of workshops, and a dozen vendors.
  • Plans capital as it goes, not once at kickoff. Every million committed is graded against a business return the PMO can see, and the next tranche is sized on that evidence.
  • Treats AI adoption as a moving standard. The roadmap will evolve as new AI capabilities land — the PMO's job is to hold the program open to them without letting any single vendor own the direction.

This is not a nice-to-have. It is the requirement of the day. A CIO initiating a Bluefield programme in 2026 who is referring a business-side PMO expert is signalling exactly that.

The first landing — a quantitative consolidation

The first objective when Lillian lands is not to run new workshops. It is to quantitatively analyse and assess the program materials already received, and consolidate them into a centralized structure that:

  • Is not tied to any one vendor's tool.
  • Is accessible from one or more AI surfaces the PMO controls.
  • Produces a shared, current picture of program state that every vendor can be measured against.

That structured repository, standing under a decision-intelligence framework, is what modern PMO practice looks like. It is what makes the difference between a program that is run and a program that is reported on.

Why the panel is set up the way it is

The Bluefield programme is initiated by the CIO organisation. IT is the business's own house in this conversation — not a third-party. What the CIO is guarding against is exactly the vendor-alignment problem above: an SI-led, vendor-shaped program where SAP's interests, the SI's channel conventions, Salesforce's roadmap, and the boutique-tech vendors' priorities each pull in a slightly different direction, and the business's capital plan drifts behind them.

Lillian is being interviewed because she is referred from the business side — most likely Finance peers who have worked with her — into a CIO-sponsored programme. The other candidates will almost certainly include at least one incumbent advisor with prior SAP-platform work at Richemont, likely associated with one of the vendors already in play, possibly an ex-internal who is now independent. That is the shape of the room. Lillian's advantage is that she is not one of them.

The two people on the panel who matter first

  • Yasmin — strategic systems advisor. She will respond to "the most strategic system you could ever imagine" — decision intelligence as the top-level operating platform for the program, not as another downstream tool bolted onto SAP. Talk to her about program-level AI, roadmap governance, and how the PMO holds the multi-vendor picture together.
  • The data lead. He will respond to being made important. Anchor the conversation on his terrain: what a structured, AI-accessible consolidation of received materials looks like, why quantitative assessment has to precede the next set of vendor asks, and how the PMO makes his function the source of truth the program runs on rather than a downstream reporting function.

Land those two first. From there the rest of the panel is easier — the CIO-staff ally who says "Lillian is the best one" is the outcome of that first conversation, not the input to it.

Five things a data-driven PMO would ask a layer below "data"

Framed for the room, not for a data workshop. Each of these is a program-governance question first, a data question second.

  1. Where does the consolidated view of the programme actually live today? Vendor decks, workshop outputs, prior-phase artifacts — what surface does the PMO query when it needs a current answer, and is it AI-accessible or is it a share drive?
  2. How is capital being graded against return, tranche by tranche? Is the next release sized on the evidence of the last one landing, or on the original plan? Where does that evidence live and who owns it?
  3. How are the vendors' interests being kept aligned to the business's outcomes? SAP, the SI, Salesforce, Cegid, Workday, Google — who is holding the seam, and against what shared measurement?
  4. Where is the client-master source-of-truth across Salesforce, Cegid, and SAP — for consent, cross-border transfer, and deletion — and how is it reconciled under GDPR, PIPL, and CCPA simultaneously?
  5. Which decisions in the roadmap are being held open for AI capabilities that do not yet exist, and which are being closed prematurely because one vendor prefers the closed option?

One option Lillian may want to run once inside

Once the consolidation is in place and the PMO is holding the multi-vendor seam, one useful move is to profile the most complex transaction on the estate — warranty & after-sales — first, and let every other client-facing volume (retail sale, clienteling, marketing journey, loyalty accrual, CPO resale) drop out as a subset of that model. After-sales carries every field every other flow needs (serialized equipment master, multi-decade lifecycle, cross-border routing across 10 Group repair workshops, three-system reconciliation, and the full regulatory retention perimeter), so profiling it first tends to make the rest easier. This is an inside move Lillian can bring to bear once she has the seat — it is not the pitch.

The closing frame

Richemont's CIO organisation is running a Bluefield programme and referring a business-side PMO expert. That referral is the tell: they want a PMO seat that can hold a group of vendors aligned to the business's capital plan, using AI as the operating instrument of program leadership, in an era where the standard for how that is done is changing every quarter.

Lillian's twist on the classic PMO hire is exactly that: a data-driven, AI-native PMO practitioner who has built exactly this kind of consolidated, AI-accessible program surface elsewhere, and who knows how to make it survive a multi-vendor delivery. That is what the room is trying to recognise. The task on Monday is to let them recognise it.
